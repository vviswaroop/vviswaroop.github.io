<p class="lead">At the start of the year, my reading plan was heavily focused on distributed systems, staff-level technical leadership, and decision hygiene. Midway through, I found myself pulled toward a different kind of system: human hardware, neural architecture, and early brain development.</p>

<p>Here are three books I’ve added to the stack for this year — and the specific questions I’m carrying into each.</p>

## Human runtime & biology

**Protocols: An Operating Manual for the Human Body — Dr. Andrew Huberman**  
Most health and productivity literature stays either too academic or overly abstract. What appeals to me here is the premise of treatable, repeatable *protocols* — concrete biological mechanics tied to actionable routines.

- **The core interest:** Circadian timing (morning light, temperature minimums), sleep architecture, and nervous system regulation (like the physiological sigh) under sustained operational pressure.
- **The systems lens:** Human physiology is an operational runtime. If the underlying hardware inputs — light, recovery cycles, dopamine management, and metabolic fuel — are misconfigured, optimizing higher-level cognitive workloads is fighting an uphill battle.

## Cognitive architecture & constraints

**Brain Rules — Dr. John Medina**  
We spend decades designing software around CPU limits, memory bottlenecks, and network boundaries, yet we often operate our own brains as if they have infinite context windows and instant context switching. Medina breaks down 12 biological principles of how the brain actually learns, recalls, and survives.

- **The core interest:** The mechanics of working memory, the role of physical movement in neurogenesis, and why stress damages the brain’s ability to process complex abstractions.
- **The systems lens:** Memory and attention have hard physiological boundaries. Understanding how biological hardware stores and retrieves patterns helps in structuring deep work, designing clearer documentation, and cutting out the cognitive friction of multitasking.

## Early development & neuroplasticity

**Raise a Smarter Child by Kindergarten — Dr. David Perlmutter & Carol Colman**  
Last year, reading *The Montessori Baby* changed how I approached the physical environment at home — opting for prepared floor spaces, low shelves, and fewer toys. Perlmutter takes this further through a neurological lens, exploring the explosive period of neuroplasticity between birth and age five.

- **The core interest:** The physiological foundations of early cognition — nutritional inputs (DHA, gut-brain microbiome), sensory-rich active play over passive screens, and the compounding power of early language exposure.
- **The systems lens:** Early childhood is the ultimate bootstrapping phase. The synaptic connections formed and pruned before kindergarten establish the baseline for curiosity, emotional regulation, and cognitive resilience later in life.

## Why this shift matters

Looking back at the [original 2026 list](/blog/2026-01-06-books-to-read-in-2026/), the focus was largely on the external: platforms, influence without authority, and system boundaries.

These three books turn the lens inward:

1. **Hardware before software:** Discipline and strategic thinking rely directly on physical vitality, sleep, and circadian rhythm.
2. **Respect the biological boundaries:** The human brain is not a cloud server; it thrives on focused intervals, recovery, and movement.
3. **Compound interest starts early:** In engineering, foundational architecture choices dictate technical debt for years. In parenting and cognition, early neurodevelopmental scaffolding works the exact same way.
